# Digital Technologies in SHM

## Introduction

As a humanities major studying heritage management with sustainability being a central point, there are several digital technologies that we use as tools for when we preserve and interpret the past. As mentioned in the section on Sustainable Heritage Management, this field has a responsibility to both preserve and protect the past in the forms of tangible and intangible heritage, as well as safeguarding the meanings, identities, relationships between different communities and their heritage. In this context, digital technologies are not neutral instruments, as their functions, limitations, and usage, as well as the persons operating or controlling the tool, influence what is documented, how it is represented, and who has access to it. These challenges are also a key component to examine, when it comes to how we go about managing certain heritage. <br>
Over, especially, the past decade, digital tools have become increasingly embedded and integral to heritage management. Since this is an extensive field that encompasses practices from so many other related fields such as archaeology, anthropology, and museum studies, it is perhaps no surprise that the technologies, digital or analogue, are much alike. 

## Current Digital Technologies in SHM

Because of the interdisciplinary nature of this field, there are a plethora of different types of digital technologies used for many different purposes. For this essay, I will be focussing on two prominent parts of the field, mainly the documentation of heritage and the interpretation of heritage, examining some of the more commonly used digital technologies herein.

### Digital Documentation Technologies

One of the most established and recognised uses of digital technology in heritage management is digital documentation. This is where we see techniques such as 3D laser scanning, photogrammetry, and drone photography, as such techniques allow us to create and recreate highly detailed records of sites and objects of heritage significance, especially immovable heritage such as buildings, archeological sites, and vast landscapes. Rather than having to rely on written descriptions or regular photographs, heritage professionals can create digital representations that capture the physical characteristics of a site in extreme detail. These 3D images are used for several purposes, particularly for research, conservation, monitoring, restoration, and education. For example, at the archeological site of Umm Qais in Jordan a research team spend three years making laser scans and digital surveying of the area, which they eventually used to record and model the site, and the resulting digital material they got from that was connected with local community involvement and public engagement (Selim, 2024). This example is interesting to highlight for these reasons; the technology is both used to produce digital copies of the site, as well as a tool of documentation that engages the participation of people connected to this land in the process of interpretion and education of the site.

![image](3D_UmmQais.png "Image of the Byzantine monuments generated from a 3D point cloud. Image from Selim, G., Jamhawi, M., Abdelmonem, M. G., Ma’bdeh, S., & Holland, A. (2022). The Virtual Living Museum.")

From a sustainability standpoint, these methods of documentation are greatly regarded. Most heritage sites, if not all, are vulnerable to many forms of change and degradation from internal and external factors, such as climate change, natural disasters, conflics and wars, tourism, and urban development. Creating these detailed digital records also means that information about the a site can be retained even in case of future damage to the physical heritage. So in this sense, digital documentation can function as a form of cultural preservation. At the same time, it can make conservation and research work less dependent on physical access to the heritage, aiding researchers and heritage professionals as they can still study the digital model extensively without having to rely on travel or physically interacting with a fragile object or structure. <br>
Another important thing for heritage professionals to remember about 3D models and digital reconstructions is to not confuse them with the heritage itself. While a 3D model can very accurately reproduce the physical elements of a piece of tangible heritage, it cannot make a stand in for all the things that give it its meaning. It cannot capture the memories associated with it, the experiences of people who use it, or any of the cultural and political meanings that have developed around it. As Laurajane Smith (2006) argues, heritage is fundamentally a cultural and social process. Digitization can therefore be a vessel for preserving certain information about heritage, without necessarily preserving the heritage in its entirety.

### Digital Interpretation Technologies

The importance of digital technologies become even more clear when considering how we in many ways have come to depend on it for how we interpret heritage for the public. Something that has become increasingly popular to use in museums and at heritage sites on the communication and interpretation side is Virtual Reality (VR) and Augmented Reality (AR). VR can allow visitors to explore historical environments that no longer exist, while AR can add layers of historical information to places as they are experienced in the present (Basheer et al., 2023). For example, a research team created a VR experience for tourists in Macao for the Ruins of Saint Paul’s (Su, et al., 2023.). They recorded the site with specialized cameras to create a digital twin of the heritage, which they then used to create an app for tourists to explore the site through.

![image](macaotourVR.png "The functions of an app created for a VR experience of different heritage locations in Macau. Image from Su, P. et al. (2023). Investigating the Relationship between Users’ Behavioral Intentions and Learning Effects of VR System for Sustainable Tourism Development.")

One of the strengths of VR and AR is that they provide easy access to heritage that is geographically distant, inaccessible for various reasons, or is just too fragile to accommodate a great number of tourists. A clear indicator of this became apparent during the COVID-19 pandemic, when travel was prohibited and discouraged. Museums and cultural institutions increasingly experimented with online and virtual forms of engagement. Virtual heritage experiences can also contribute to accessibility as they provide opportunities for people who are otherwise unable to engage in physical participation. In this sense, digital interpretation can contribute to a more inclusive experience of heritage.

## Conclusion

It is clear that digital technologies are and have been changing sustainable heritage management in ways beyond just making heritage more easy to document and store a digital copy of. 3D scanning and digital modelling can create detailed records of vulnerable heritage, while VR and AR can be used to create new ways of experiencing heritage. And while these technologies are embedded more and more into heritage management, we should remember to not use them as replacements of the actual heritage we are trying to manage.
